Title Slide

* Good Evening, my name is Roni Pitkowsky. My presentation is titled 'Persistence of Racial Disparities in Debt Burden.'
* I examined whether racial differences in high debt burden reflect individual socioeconomic characteristics—or structural barriers that persist even when households have similar resources.
* My analysis shows that racial minorities face higher odds of high debt burden even when they have the same wealth, education, and family structure as White households—pointing to barriers built into the financial system itself.

Key Finding

* State the implication clearly:

  * This suggests that debt burden isn't just about who has less—it reflects structural barriers that affect households even when they have similar economic resources.

Transition: So why does it matter whether we view debt burden as structural or individual?



Why This Matters

* Challenge the dominant narrative:

  * Most policy responses focus on individual solutions: financial literacy classes, credit counseling, promoting homeownership.
  * These assume debt burden stems from poor choices or lack of knowledge.
* Your evidence contradicts this:

  * In my analysis, education wasn't significant after controls.
  * The homeownership effect disappeared after accounting for wealth.
  * But race remained significant—suggesting something beyond individual factors is at work.
* Introduce the structural lens (briefly name Wherry):

  * This aligns with Frederick Wherry's concept of 'viral debt': debt circulates through racialized pathways shaped by historical exclusion and policy design.
  * Discriminatory lending, unequal credit access, intergenerational wealth gaps—these aren't captured by standard economic indicators.
* One-sentence takeaway:

  * If debt burden is structural, then individual-focused interventions may miss the deeper drivers.



Transition: Let me briefly walk through how I tested this.



Data \& Method

* Data source (keep it simple):

  * I used the 2022 Survey of Consumer Finances—a nationally representative survey of 4,595 U.S. households facilitated by the Federal Reserve.
  * The SCF provides detailed data on assets, debts, income, and demographics.
* Outcome variable:

  * High debt burden is defined as a Payment-to-Income Ratio above 40%—a conventional threshold for financial distress.
  * This includes mortgage payments, student loans, auto loans, and credit cards.
* Key predictors:

  * I examined race/ethnicity, wealth quintiles, education, marital status, homeownership, presence of children, and age.
* Method (plain language):

  * I used survey-weighted logistic regression to estimate the independent effect of each predictor.
  * Survey-weighted means the results account for the SCF's complex design—replicate weights and multiple imputation.
  * Results are reported as odds ratios: values above 1 indicate higher odds of high debt burden.
* Analytic strategy:

  * First, I looked at raw bivariate patterns.
  * Then, I added controls to test whether racial disparities persist after accounting for socioeconomic factors.



Transition: Now let me walk you through what the data shows



Visual evidence

* Introduce the race plot (point to left panel):

  * On the left, you see the raw bivariate pattern: White households have the highest rate of high debt burden at 11.6%.

* Reveal the adjusted pattern (point to right panel):

  * But after controlling for wealth, education, and other factors, the picture flips.
  * Asian households face 2.8 times the odds of high debt burden compared to White households.

* Explain the "flip" briefly:

  * The raw pattern reflected who has access to formal debt—mortgages, student loans, credit cards.
  * The adjusted pattern reveals who faces underlying risk independent of those resources.



* Connect to wealth plot

  * We also see a clear wealth gradient: households in the top 10% have 75% lower odds of high debt burden compared to the bottom quartile.
  * This protective effect persists after controls, confirming that asset position matters independently.
* One-sentence synthesis:

  * Together, these patterns support a structural interpretation: racialized pathways shape financial vulnerability beyond individual characteristics.



Transition: Let me conclude by connecting these findings to policy implications.



Conclusion

* Restate the core finding:

  * Racial disparities in high debt burden persist after controlling for wealth, education, and family structure.
  * This suggests structural barriers—not just individual deficits—drive financial fragility.
* Theoretical contribution:

  * These findings support Wherry's framework: debt circulates through racialized pathways, not randomly.
  * Historical exclusion, discriminatory lending, and wealth gaps create conditions in which financial strain concentrates disproportionately.
* Policy implications:

  * Policies should help households escape the bottom wealth quartile—where the protective effect of assets is strongest—through tools like matched savings accounts or down payment assistance.
  * Because race matters independently of wealth or education, policies should directly address racialized barriers
* Closing statement:

  * Reducing financial fragility requires addressing structural pathways, not just symptoms.
